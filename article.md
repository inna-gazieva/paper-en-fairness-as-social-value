## Abstract

*(Official English abstract as published by the journal.)*

The aim of the article is to provide a sociological definition of fairness in the context of
state policy to strengthen traditional Russian morals based on the results of using the
author's empirical model of analyzing the students' perception regarding social values
(Decree of the President of the Russian Federation No. 809 dated 11/09/2022).

*Methods.* The theoretical and methodological basis of the research is an interdisciplinary
synthesis of philosophical concepts of fairness and sociological theories of values, which
made it possible to identify four dimensions of fairness value, such as ideal, desired,
expected and real. Empirical testing was carried out on a representative sample of RANEPA
students (N = 4338) as part of the online survey "Traditional and strategic students' values"
(May–June 2023) by using a linear distribution analysis and correlation analysis (Spearman
coefficient) of the sociological data.

*Results.* The present study confirms integrity of fairness as a social value. The author
revealed a high internal consistency of all its components (from the meritocratic ideal to
real behavior). Furthermore, the systemic mutual relationship between fairness and
responsibility, humanity, labor and cognitive activity is established. It became the basis
for the operational definition of the concept under study, which now allows considering
fairness not as an isolated attitude, but as the core of a value structure integrating
personal and social orientations.

*Discussion.* The author proposes a sociological definition of justice as a socially
significant universal, manifested in a consistent system of attitudes and practices
identified by an empirical model of social values analysis. The results are of direct
practical relevance. It lies in providing an empirical model for monitoring the traditional
value interiorization among young people and developing value education focused not on
declarative assimilation of norms, but on the formation of an internally consistent value
position that can become the basis for building a just society by means of personal
responsibility and ethic interaction.

**Keywords:** sociology of values, fairness, social justice, sociology of youth, social
values, values, Russian traditional values

## Introduction

"All say: there is no justice on earth. But there is no justice higher up, either." The
immortal opening line of A. S. Pushkin's play *Mozart and Salieri* reads like an epigraph to
this article. Concentrated in this phrase are, at once, the search for justice and the
statement of its absence, the pain of — and disagreement with — this state of affairs in
social practice, which troubled the minds of the philosophers of antiquity and remains
topical today.

In contemporary society, undergoing profound transformations, the question of justice — which
serves simultaneously as a yardstick for evaluating the existing order and as an ideal toward
which society strives — becomes not merely an ethical but a political and social challenge.
Justice, like any social phenomenon, is a concept that is not only subjective but also highly
ambiguous, which shows in society's ambivalent attitude toward it. Thus, according to a VCIOM
survey, 47% of respondents consider contemporary Russian society to be on the whole justly
organized (the sum of the answers "definitely yes" and "rather yes"), and exactly as many —
47% of respondents — disagree with this statement (the sum of the answers "definitely no" and
"rather no").[^1] Such figures reflect ambiguity not only in the assessments of the composite
concept of justice itself but also in the understanding of justice's constituent elements.
This evident ambivalence underscores at once the power and the paradox of justice as a social
phenomenon that calls for detailed study and substantive analysis.

[^1]: VCIOM monitoring "Social Justice" (13–14 April 2025), N = 1600 //
    <https://wciom.ru/analytical-reviews/analiticheskii-obzor/socialnaja-spravedlivost-monitoring-07.05.2025>

As we can see, in everyday life justice is a subjective, evaluative category, one that "in
general terms prescribes the correspondence of the real to the due, and which generates a
multitude of scenarios for the organization of a just society" (Barash, 2024, p. 98), and it
may be perceived differently by individuals and groups of individuals in different cultures
and in different situations.

As for scholarly approaches, here too no significant clarity or terminological unity can be
traced, even in the definition of the generic concept. Today, in contemporary humanities
scholarship, justice as a social phenomenon is receiving ever greater attention, which is
reflected both in its broadest interpretations — as "a general moral characteristic of
people's life together" that "manifests itself primarily in the collision of their interests
and desires" (Gorshkov, 2022, pp. 34–35), and one that "gives form to the connection between
the character of order in a collective or society and spiritual and moral principles"
(Volkov, 2022, p. 28) — and in narrower interpretations focused on specific spheres of social
relations: as a regulator, for example, of inter-gender relations (Milovanova, Irsetskaya,
2023) or of inter-ethnic relations, constituting "a value reference point and a principle of
regulation that provides legitimation and social control with respect to various patterns of
interaction of ethnic groups, and of ethnic groups and social institutions" (Volkov, Bineeva,
2023, p. 115). In addition, an article by E. A. Vasilyeva presents the results of a rather
detailed study of this concept from the standpoint of its use in scholarly discourse, on the
basis of which several of its meanings are proposed: "an equivalent of the concept of
'equality'"; "a criterion of law-abidingness"; "a factor of normativity ensuring social
stability"; "a criterion of the acceptability of the distribution of social goods"; "the
highest virtue, determined by the moral and ethical attitudes of society" (Vasilyeva, 2024,
p. 381). It must be noted, however, that justice specifically as a value is not mentioned
there.

What brings us closer to understanding justice in the value context proper is the approach of
A. B. Weber who, with respect to the development of society and the economy, proposes
adhering to an objective criterion of social justice, meaning at a minimum the following:
equal starting opportunities for individuals, as well as society's support for those whose
opportunities to achieve the desired results are limited for reasons beyond their control.
Inequality of starting opportunities and objectively conditioned differences in life
circumstances are naturally perceived as injustice (Weber, 2015).

A reflection of this approach can also be found in contemporary international research which,
in our view, is thematically (in its approaches) diametrically opposite. It deals, in
essence, with professional and labor justice, where justice is studied in the context of
labor, employment, and the professions. Much attention is paid to Social Justice Education
(SJE) as a learning process aimed at developing the understanding and skills needed to combat
discrimination and inequality in society through the analysis of power relations, privilege,
and systemic problems. Articles by international colleagues actively investigate human
perceptions of justice in access to work, in the distribution of responsibilities, and in the
opportunity to realize one's potential: justice is realized through the involvement of
members of the professional community in the choice of activity, respect for their rights,
and the creation of conditions for self-determined work and leisure (Bailliard, Dallman,
Carroll, Lee et al., 2020); an orientation toward social justice in the educational process
is emphasized, where teachers' "multiculturalist" beliefs contribute to the creation of a
socially just educational environment (Rissanen, Kuusisto, Timm, Kaukko, 2022), including
through the justification of educational commitments (Sanjakdar, Premier, 2022), and so on.
It is evident from this that justice in the professional sphere is not merely a "fair wage"
but access to meaningful, decent activity. It is important to note that contemporary
researchers here shift the emphasis from economic equality to participation, inclusion, and
self-determination; theoretically, this develops the ideas of M. Nussbaum (the capability
approach) (Nussbaum, 2013) and J. Rawls (the difference principle) (Rawls, 1995, p. 303).

Despite the considerable number of theoretical and empirical studies devoted to justice in
its various meanings and contexts, we nevertheless find no explicit reference to the study of
justice as a value in its own proper meaning — which is of great interest to us, since
justice is among the traditional Russian values enshrined in Decree of the President of the
Russian Federation No. 809 of 9 November 2022 "On Approving the Foundations of State Policy
for the Preservation and Strengthening of Traditional Russian Spiritual and Moral Values."

This state of affairs in social practice determined the purpose of this article, which is to
develop a sociological definition of justice on the basis of the results of using the
author's empirical model of the analysis of social values as perceived by student youth, in
the context of state policy for strengthening traditional Russian spiritual and moral values
(Decree of the President of the Russian Federation No. 809 of 9 November 2022).

## Methods and materials. A logical analysis of the concept of justice

Let us turn first of all to the definition of the generic concept of justice which, owing to
its interdisciplinary character, is not self-evident. It is generally held that justice
originally meant only "subjective conformity to the law in force" (iustitia) (Höffe, 2007,
p. 10); today, justice is firmly enshrined as one of the principles of international and
Russian jurisprudence in key international normative legal acts (for example, the Universal
Declaration of Human Rights, 1948, art. 10; the European Convention for the Protection of
Human Rights and Fundamental Freedoms, 1950, art. 6 (1), etc.), as well as in the
Constitution of the Russian Federation (art. 12) and in all codes of the Russian Federation.
The phenomenon of justice is disclosed and used as a principle in its applied, sectoral
meaning, serving as a principle of professional activity. For example, the principle of
justice underlies any system of evaluation — from the functioning of the justice system to
the organization of the learning process. Defining justice as a principle is necessary and
sufficient for the field of law and jurisprudence, but it is insufficient for a sociological
definition.

Within the contemporary international humanities research mentioned earlier, justice is most
often considered not in its own proper meaning but in the narrower, focused meaning of
"social justice," understood not as an abstract ethical principle but as an active,
reflexive, and transformative practice aimed at overcoming systemic inequalities, ensuring
equal access to education, and developing critical consciousness in learners and educators
(see: Kelly, Adrian van Breda, 2025; Le, Staley, Shore, Nutton, 2024; Sanjakdar, Premier,
2023). An analysis of the available materials shows that in the academic environment,
especially in the sphere of initial teacher education (ITE), social justice is becoming one
of the social elements of the foundation of the educational process.

In the literature covering healthcare, labor, housing, technology, peacebuilding, and
sustainable development, overlapping systems of exclusion and structural inequality are
repeatedly emphasized (Düzgün, Sharma, Taran, 2025, p. 33). In all these areas justice is
consistently formulated not only as a normative ideal but also as a practical program
requiring institutional, legal, and political transformations.

On the whole, it would seem that justice should be understood, and its study approached, as a
multidimensional and integrative category. Turning to the philosophical tradition, which lays
a bridge to the sociological interpretation of the concept of justice, allows us to view this
phenomenon more broadly. Thus, for example, in Aristotle justice is defined at once as a
virtue (Aristotle, 1129b, pp. 1–2) and as a certain characteristic of the equal distribution
of goods (Aristotle, 1130b, pp. 30–31); for J. Rawls (Rawls, 1995, p. 19) justice is "the
first virtue of social institutions," a moral property of character; for O. Höffe justice
constitutes "the probity of a person" (Höffe, 2007, p. 10). The Cuban philosopher Félix
Varela, who also considered justice a virtue, discloses it through the following components:
"piety, observance of the commandments, truthfulness, generosity, friendship" — while
emphasizing that "under certain circumstances it is permissible to conceal the truth if its
disclosure may cause harm to an individual, to those close to them, or to society as a whole"
(Suárez, 2015, p. 77). In the contemporary sociological literature we more often encounter
reflections on this concept in general, without reference to any specific generic concept: as
a certain characteristic, "one of the traditional categories rooted in Russian culture,
reflecting the deep aspirations of the Russian people" (Chernysh, 2021, p. 530), as "a notion
of the due" (Volkov, 1998, p. 530), and as "a general moral sanction of people's life
together" (Guseinov, 2001, pp. 536–538), and so on. S. Schwartz, in his Theory of Basic Human
Values, includes justice (social justice) in the cluster of the motivational type of
universalism, alongside equality and beauty in the surrounding world, emphasizing its role as
a motivational goal that determines the choice and evaluation of social practices (Schwartz,
Butenko, Sedova, Lipatova, 2012, p. 50).

It is evident that justice is indeed so polysemous a category that it is impossible to settle
on one or even several definitions for its analysis. Moreover, this category is so sensitive
to the particularities of the social environment that it cannot always be defined for certain
specific stretches of historical time, since its very existence at those moments is doubtful
— a point emphasized by M. F. Chernysh, who relies on the absence of the term from V. Dahl's
dictionary: "…and this is no accident: in an estate society, where people are unequal by
birth and by their initial condition, there can be no justice" (Chernysh, 2021, pp. 62–63).
In this vein, the sociologist speaks of the greater popularity and currency, in the cultural
space of Russian society, of the categories of truth (pravda) and faith (Chernysh, 2021,
pp. 59, 62).

This state of affairs leads us all the more convincingly to the need to study the phenomenon
of justice by drawing on a broad spectrum of scholarly approaches.

Thus, some of the earliest reflections on the essence and meaning of justice are found in the
dialogues of Socrates as recorded by Plato in the *Republic*. Here the great thinker gives no
single definition of justice but, by the method of maieutics — questioning and refuting the
definitions proposed by others — makes his interlocutors ponder the substantive
contradictoriness of the concept of justice, its ambivalence. He leads his interlocutors away
from its seemingly obvious meaning — "to render to each their due: good to friends and evil
to enemies" (Cephalus) — toward doubt as to "whether justice is something that benefits
friends and harms enemies" (Socrates), concluding that "justice is a quality of the soul that
makes it better… while that which makes the soul worse cannot be the work of the just"
(Plato, I, 332a–353e). The thinker thereby shows that justice is not simple retribution but a
quality of the soul directed toward the good and the perfection of the person, since to harm
even an enemy is to destroy the very essence of justice.

Plato himself speaks of three dimensions of justice: "justice in the state (…) — when each
does their own work and does not meddle in what belongs to others" (Plato, IV, 433a–b);
"justice in the person (…) — when reason rules, the will assists it, and the appetites obey"
(Plato, IV, 441d–442a); and justice as the embodiment of "the health, beauty, and good of the
soul" (Plato, IV, 444e). In essence, the first case concerns social justice, grounded in
social order and the division of labor; the second concerns universal human justice, seen in
the inner harmony of three components — reason, will, and "appetites" ("the just person is
one in whom reason governs, the will stands guard, and bodily desires are moderated" (Plato,
IV, 442c)); in the third case justice is the highest virtue, which is not merely a moral rule
but a condition of the happiness and well-being of the soul.

From this we see that the concept of "justice" is indeed a value category that "makes it
possible to judge the existing social order" and is at the same time connected with "the
moral consciousness of society, the notions of good and evil shared by the majority of
society's members" (Butenko, 2019, p. 114). In the opinion of G. L. Tulchinsky, alongside
security and, in part, freedom, it is one of the basic values of sociogenesis, occupying "a
key role in the value vector of the dynamics of society's development" (Tulchinsky, 2017,
p. 58). It is therefore evident that "the concept of 'justice,' like many other concepts that
sometimes seem eternal and imperishable, proves on inspection to be historically conditioned,
instrumentally bound to the existing social order" (Chernysh, 2021, pp. 59–82, 79).

It is evident that justice as a value is not merely a notion of "the due" but a social
regulator of interactions that ensures stability in society and the legitimacy of governance
processes. Furthermore, on the basis of the theoretical analysis carried out, one can say
that justice is not merely a moral ideal or a legal principle but a social value, since it
performs three key functions: a regulative function — setting reference points for evaluation
and behavior; an integrative function — securing agreement in the distribution of recognition
and responsibility; and a motivational function — prompting actions that correspond to the
ideal of justice.

These results enable us to proceed to an empirical study of justice as a social value on the
basis of the empirical model of the analysis of social values developed and described in
detail by the author earlier (see: Gazieva, 2024; Gazieva, 2025). Let us recall that the
model rests on four categories and four indicative characteristics corresponding to each of
them, which jointly describe the empirical model of the analysis of a social value; a brief
description is presented in Table 1.

**Table 1 — An empirical model of social value analysis**

| Category | Indicative characteristic / attribute | Manifestation |
|---|---|---|
| The Ideal | Value relation to social reality | The notion of the best in general: an abstract statement about the most important features of any social phenomenon; the statement: "that which is ideal for society" |
| The Desired | Value preference (significance) | Preferences based on inclinations (justification of the choice between possible decisions): what is significant in relation to building a career; the statement: "we want"; "it is significant for us" |
| The Expected | Value attitude (readiness) | Specific intentions, social attitudes: readiness to react to the object of the relation; what a person is prepared to do for the sake of a specific value; the statement: "I am ready" |
| The Real | Value action | The actual manifestation of the relation to a value in action: what a person is doing now within a specific value; the statement: "what is in fact the case" |

To use this model in the empirical study of justice as a social value, indicators must be
defined for each category of assessment: the ideal, the desired, the expected, and the real.
Note that all indicators are included in the sociological questionnaire in the form of
judgments, with each of which respondents express their agreement or disagreement on a Likert
scale.

Let us begin with the ideal. Since justice is "a notion of the due, containing a requirement
of correspondence between: the practical role of various individuals (social strata, groups)
in the life of society and their social position; their rights and their duties; labor and
remuneration; merits and their public recognition; crime and punishment" (Volkov, 1998,
p. 530), we formulate the judgment in the category of the ideal as: "The quality of a
person's life depends on that person's own actions."

The next judgment — in the category of the desired — is formulated in the context of the
approaches of Aristotle and J. Rawls, both of whom regard justice as a measure of the
distribution of goods and recognition. For Rawls justice is "the first virtue of social
institutions," while for Aristotle it is a virtue of the person manifested in actions. In
this context the indicative judgment is formulated as follows: "It is important for me to
have a just assessment of my actions."

Both of the approaches mentioned make it possible to move from abstraction to measurable
behavioral indicators not only of the desired but also of the expected. Moreover, since
"justice must become a criterion of social development and of the formation of the social
order in society" (Nikovskaya, 2017, p. 17), the indicative judgment for the category of the
expected is formulated as: "I am ready to receive a low assessment if I deserve it."

According to T. Parsons's conception of the social system, values are "notions of the desired
type of social system that regulate the processes whereby acting subjects assume certain
obligations" (Parsons, 1998, pp. 17–21); accordingly, values constitute generally accepted
symbolic reference points that secure the cohesion of society. Justice, being such a value,
makes it possible to reconcile individual interests and collective norms. The judgment in the
category of the real is therefore formulated as follows: "I am usually objective in my
assessments and characterizations of people's actions." This formulation of the judgment may
appear socially desirable; let us not forget, however, the supreme subjectivity of justice as
such, for "the way things are does not determine the way they ought to be" (Sandel, 2013,
p. 169), which makes formulations in a different key practically impossible and might also
contradict the conditions of use of our empirical model. Moreover, the use of various methods
of statistical analysis makes it possible to avoid subjectivity of conclusions based on the
results of the analysis of the sociological data obtained.

For the purpose of empirically testing the approach thus formed to the study of justice as a
social value, the indicative judgments formulated above were included in a single sociological
toolkit (a sociological questionnaire) used for the comprehensive study of traditional
Russian values within the online study "Traditional and Strategic Values of Student
Youth,"[^2] conducted by the author from 17 May to 1 June 2023 among full-time RANEPA
students (N = 4338) through their personal student accounts (89% of respondents are enrolled
in bachelor's programs and 11% in master's programs, evenly distributed across years and
regions of study; 64.5% are women and 35.5% are men — in proportion to the gender structure
of the RANEPA student body). Note that we do not extrapolate the results of the study either
to the entire general population of student youth or to any broader community. Our task was
to test the developed approach as applied to the study of justice as a value, and to propose
a refined definition of the concept of "justice."

The questionnaire includes indicators of all four categories for assessing the level of

President of the Russian Federation No. 809, and additionally of the values set out in the
Methodological Recommendations of the Ministry of Science and Higher Education of the Russian
Federation.[^3]

properly sociological meaning of justice as a social value.

## Results

### An empirical analysis of justice as a social value

Let us first analyze the simple linear distributions of respondents' assessments of the
indicative judgments devoted directly to justice, whose formulations we described earlier.

It should be noted that, since we had planned from the outset to process the results of the
study using various methods of statistical analysis, a nine-point scale possessing high
sensitivity was used to assess respondents' opinions of the value judgments. However, for
simplicity of analyzing the linear distributions and clarity in presenting the results, this
scale was converted by recoding into a three-point scale, whereby scale values in the range
of 1–3 points correspond to 1 point, 4–6 to 2 points, and 7–9 to 3 points.

Let us now turn directly to the results of the analysis of our sociological study, according
to which (see Table 2) a high degree of consensus among respondents regarding justice is
evident at all levels of its assessment (across all four categories). Thus, on average, the
absolute majority of respondents (more than 78%) completely agree with all four indicative
judgments, which points to the wide currency of the idea of justice as a value among student
youth and demonstrates the internal consistency of their value position — from the ideal to
real behavior.

**Table 2 — Distribution of respondents' agreement with indicative judgments characterizing the category "justice", %**

| Categories | Indicative judgments | 1 — Completely disagree | 2 — Agree by half | 3 — Completely agree | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| The Ideal | The quality of a person's life depends on that person's own actions | 2.4 | 18.9 | 78.7 | 100 |
| The Desired | To have a just assessment of my actions | 1.1 | 5.1 | 93.8 | 100 |
| The Expected | I am ready to receive a low assessment if I deserve it | 3.4 | 13.6 | 83 | 100 |
| The Real | I am usually objective in my assessments and characterizations of people's actions | 2.6 | 10.4 | 87 | 100 |

Especially noteworthy is the fact that the judgments in the categories of "the desired" and
"the real" received the highest agreement figures (93.8% and 87.0% respectively), which
demonstrates a high level of perception of justice not only as an abstract ideal but as a
personally significant practice in which respondents see themselves as active participants.

Attention should also be paid to the fact that the statement "The quality of a person's life
depends on that person's own actions" (78.7% completely agree), which reflects a certain
meritocratic attitude — the belief that success and well-being are earned rather than
accidental or imposed by structure — represents an attitude that may mask structural
inequalities if the influence of social origin, access to resources, and systemic barriers is
ignored.

According to the results of the analysis, respondents' high need for recognition is also
evident: "I want to be assessed honestly." Thus, the figure of 93.8% in the category of "the
desired" is the maximum among all categories of our empirical model, which demonstrates the
obviousness of the perception of just assessment as a basic social need; furthermore, there
is evidently a strong motivation for recognition of one's merits, and a possibly high
sensitivity of respondents to unjust assessment (for example, within the educational
process).

Respondents' readiness for self-sanction deserves separate attention, being a sign of a
certain social maturity. The rather high level of "the expected" (83% agree with the
readiness to accept a low assessment) constitutes a key indicator of the internal integration
of justice: the individual not only wants to be assessed honestly but also recognizes the
right of others to criticism when it is well-founded. Such a position is rare in cultures
with a widespread "cult of success." Its presence speaks to a developed reflexive capacity,
an internal autonomy of moral judgment, and a reduction of the cognitive dissonance between
"I want recognition" and "I am ready for criticism."

The final indicator, characterizing respondents' real behavior on the basis of their
self-assessment, shows that the absolute majority of them (87%) note frequent instances of
their own objectivity in assessing others. A social desirability effect is certainly possible
here, since people tend to present themselves as more just than they actually are; however,
the results of the correlation analysis presented below (see Table 3) make it possible to
assume that this is not a mere declaration but a reflection of a real position — especially
among student youth, where self-control, the beginnings of professionalism, and an ethic of
interaction are valued.

For the purpose of a more detailed analysis of the value of justice and the identification of
its connections with the other social values studied in the course of the full study, a
correlation analysis was carried out using Spearman's method (Table 3). The table includes
only those indicative value judgments that have the highest correlations with all four
indicators of the value of justice.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. The Desired: It is important for me to bear responsibility for the results of my actions | 0.416 | 0.718 | 0.438 | 0.440 |

| 3. The Desired: It is important for me to be able to provide help when necessary | 0.341 | 0.550 | 0.400 | 0.440 |
| 4. The Expected: I am ready to behave humanely and respectfully toward everyone | 0.338 | 0.505 | 0.479 | 0.471 |
| 5. The Expected: I am ready to work actively and hard in order to have decent professional experience | 0.365 | 0.427 | 0.430 | 0.419 |
| 6. The Expected: I am ready to learn, to seek out and discover new things | 0.365 | 0.494 | 0.446 | 0.450 |
| 7. The Expected: I am ready to work actively and hard in order to realize my professional competencies and abilities | 0.373 | 0.458 | 0.424 | 0.451 |

The first thing that draws attention is the presence of two of the four indicators of the
value of responsibility among those correlating with the indicators of the value of justice,
which indicates that justice is most strongly connected precisely with responsibility. Let us
look more closely at the data obtained.

Responsibility for the results of one's own actions ("It is important for me to bear
responsibility for the results of my actions") is most closely connected with the desire to
be justly assessed (0.718), which indicates that personal responsibility and just assessment
are perceived as two sides of the same coin: if I answer for the result, I deserve an honest
assessment.

As for the readiness to bear responsibility for one's actions, this is one of the strongest
correlates of justice after the previous one (0.634). The readiness to accept the
consequences of one's deeds is directly connected with the desire to be justly assessed and
with the real striving for objectivity, which points to the integration of justice into the
individual's personal structure: the person not only wants to be assessed honestly but is
also ready to pay an honest "price" for their mistakes.

Let us next consider the level of readiness to help others (an indicator of the value of
mutual assistance: "It is important for me to be able to provide help when necessary"). Here
the connection with the value of justice is moderate but most pronounced with the desire to
receive a just assessment (0.550), which may signify the perception of helping others as an
action deserving recognition, in which the person — the actor of these actions — expects that
their efforts will be noticed and assessed on their merits.

The next group of correlations, which includes an indicator of the value of humanism ("I am
ready to behave humanely and respectfully toward everyone"), demonstrates a moderate but
quite stable connection with the indicators of the value of justice (0.505); this indicates
that justice encompasses not only the assessment of merits but also a moral attitude toward
the other person as a personality.

A single group of indicative judgments characterizing values that are not traditional Russian
values but are recommended by the Ministry of Education of the Russian Federation for use in
organizing youth policy at universities — jointly reflecting respondents' labor and cognitive
activity (readiness to work for the sake of decent experience; readiness to learn and
discover new things; readiness to work for the sake of realizing competencies and abilities)
— shows moderate correlations with all four aspects of justice (in the range of 0.365–0.458).
These figures indicate that people who value work, self-development, and the realization of
potential also tend to support just principles in the assessment of people and results, which
may reflect a meritocratic attitude: "Those who work and develop should receive commensurate
recognition and reward."

## Discussion

The empirical data obtained as a result of the sociological study conducted by the author
testify to a high degree of internal integration of justice as a social value among
respondents. The value manifests itself not only at the level of ideal attitudes (a
meritocratic worldview) but also in the motivational sphere (the desire for just assessment),
in reflexive readiness (the acceptance of criticism), and in self-reported behavior
(objectivity in assessing others), which makes it possible to regard justice not as a
situational judgment but as a stable value performing regulative, identificational, and
ethical functions simultaneously.

The closest connection is observed between the values of responsibility and justice in both
desired and real behavior, which speaks to a high inter-value consistency among respondents.
The central element of justice may be identified as the link between responsibility and
assessment: people who are ready to answer for their actions and for the results of those
actions strive most strongly for just assessments, are ready to accept negative assessments
as well, and consider themselves objective with respect to others.

Furthermore, justice is rooted in the active position of the personality: work,
self-development, helping others, humaneness. These qualities do not merely accompany justice
— they realize it in everyday life. People in whom the qualities listed (responsibility,
industriousness, humaneness) are developed simultaneously demonstrate a more mature
understanding of justice, encompassing both ideal attitudes and real behavior. The key
conclusion that can be drawn from the results of the study, which characterize a specific
sample (student youth), is that for student youth justice constitutes not a passive ideal but
an active, reflexive, and behaviorally realized position rooted in responsibility and the
striving for objectivity.

Proceeding from the presented results of the theoretical and empirical analysis of justice, a
clearer sociological definition of this social phenomenon can be proposed: justice as a
social value is a socially significant universal expressed in a consistent system of
attitudes and practices, encompassing: an ideal belief in the correspondence of personal
responsibility to quality of life (the meritocratic ideal); a desired need for an objective,
merited assessment of one's own actions; a reflexive readiness to accept negative
consequences when one's deeds fall short of the relevant criteria; and real behavior
manifested in the striving for objectivity and impartiality in assessing others.

## Conclusion

The present study was aimed at providing a theoretical and empirical substantiation of the
status of justice as a social value, in contrast to its traditional understanding as a legal
principle, an ethical ideal, or a political slogan. In particular, justice grounded in the
correlation of notions of the due and the real is a principle, not a value. Following that
principle, however, transfers us to the plane of values.

The study conducted has shown that, theoretically, justice as a value goes back to the
Aristotelian understanding of it as a virtue, to J. Rawls's interpretation of it as "the
first virtue of social institutions," and to its contemporary sociological conceptualization
(T. Parsons, S. Schwartz, G. L. Tulchinsky, et al.), which regards it as a basic element of
the value structure of society. Furthermore, we distinguish between "social justice," which
is dialectically connected with the category of the potentially possible — realized where the
corresponding conditions (equality of basic social opportunities) obtain — and "justice" in
its own proper meaning, which has a dialectical connection with retribution, with sanctions,
as cause and effect; this is confirmed by the results of the sociological study. Empirically,
justice can manifest itself not in abstract declarations but in an integrated value position
encompassing ideals, motives, attitudes, and actions.

Since our aim was not only to record notions of justice but also to operationalize it as a
sociological concept — which we succeeded in doing by means of the author's four-level
empirical model of value assessment (the ideal, the desired, the expected, and the real),
using the study of student youth as an example — we have demonstrated that justice manifests
itself as an integral value structure rather than a set of disparate judgments. This not only
makes it possible to bridge the gap between philosophical reflection and behavioral practice
but also helps identify additional dependencies, the key one being the connection between
justice and responsibility. The specific figures obtained in the study certainly reflect the
particularities of the sample (the student youth of the Presidential Academy) and will in all
likelihood differ in studies of other socio-professional groups. We wish, however, to
emphasize the presence of specific connections and dependencies between certain values, on
the basis of which we are prepared to formulate the following practical recommendations for
the use of the model described.

The author's empirical model for the assessment of social values, which has undergone
repeated testing and was also used for the in-depth study of justice as a social value, can
be introduced into the practice of monitoring the value development of students at
universities, making it possible to identify not only the declarative attitude toward such
values as justice but also to determine the degree of their internal consistency through the
indicator of reflexive readiness and actual adherence to values.

Moreover, it is also possible to create a system for tracking the dynamics of value
consistency over the course of study (from the first year to graduation) in order to evaluate
the effectiveness of upbringing work not by the growth of declarative commitment to values
but by the strengthening of the correlations between the ideal, desired, expected, and real
levels of their manifestation.

In addition, it appears productive to focus programs of value education for student youth on
the identified "responsibility–justice" link as the core of the value structure. Given the
empirically identified extremely strong correlation between the desire for just assessment
and the readiness to bear responsibility for the results of one's actions, the values of this
pair can serve for each other both as "governing" and as "governed" values.

As for educational programs encompassing both upbringing and instruction, then — relying on
the data showing that the majority of students declare their own objectivity, thereby
creating a basis for transforming declaration into reflexive practice — it appears desirable
to introduce into some of these programs elements for developing objectivity in the
assessment of others, for example through case methods and feedback as a behavioral indicator
of justice.

The study conducted demonstrates that justice as a social value is not merely an "aspiration
of the Russian people" but a dynamic, integrated, and behaviorally active social value that
can and must become a support for building a just society not from above but from within the
personality — through the cultivation of responsibility, industriousness, and respect for the
other.
